The Fuel Mix Is a Neutron Knob

Raising the helium-3 fraction cuts neutron output sharply &mdash; almost for free in gain.

The burner's helium-3 fraction is a lever on neutron production. At the reference geometry, moving it from 0.20 to 0.30 cuts the neutron fraction from 7.2% to 4.1% while slightly raising the engineering gain — a large reduction in neutron output for essentially nothing. The frozen operating design point runs a helium-3 fraction of 0.25, at a neutron fraction of about 6.5%.

The science

A higher helium-3 fraction means fewer D-D reactions, which are the neutron source, so the neutron fraction falls steeply while the D-3He charged-particle output holds up. At the reference geometry, from 0.20 to 0.30 neutrons drop ~1.75x and gain edges up; pushing to 0.50 reaches ~1.5% neutrons but costs gain. The frozen operating point sits at 0.25 — helium-3-rich enough to close, leaving neutron output near 6.5% (frozen f_n 0.065).

Why it matters

The mix is a tuned operating choice, not luck: a leaner helium-3 fraction burns less scarce fuel but makes more neutrons, a richer one does the reverse. Publishing the whole trade curve — and naming the frozen operating point on it — is what makes the burner's low-neutron character a design decision the reader can second-guess.
